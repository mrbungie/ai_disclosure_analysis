Title: AI Washing or Credible Disclosure? Patterns and Clusters in Corporate AI Disclosures
Business Problem and Research Question
Since the release of ChatGPT, listed firms have increasingly mentioned artificial intelligence in corporate filings and investor communications. However, regulators and investors are concerned that some AI claims may be vague, promotional, or weakly connected to concrete operational capabilities. This became especially relevant in 2024, when the SEC publicly targeted "AI washing" (https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2024/03/sec-targets-ai-washing)
The managerial problem is that firms appear to adopt different AI disclosure behaviors and communication strategies, ranging from operationally grounded disclosures to more promotional or speculative narratives, while managers, investors, and regulators lack clear frameworks to distinguish between them.
Main Research Question: How do public firms differ in their AI disclosure behaviors regarding AI adoption, capabilities, risks, and governance in corporate disclosures, and what distinct disclosure archetypes or clusters emerge from these differences?
Extended Research Questions: 
How have these patterns evolved over time?
Did firms with more vague or promotional AI disclosures change their disclosure behavior after the SEC’s 2024 scrutiny of AI washing?
Did external shocks, such as DeepSeek’s emergence, affect how firms framed AI capabilities, risks, or competitive positioning?
Preliminary Literature Review
The thesis will build on recent literature on AI disclosure and market valuation. Eisfeldt, Schubert, and Zhang (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4436627) examine firm-level exposure to generative AI and its relationship with firm value. Basnet et al. analyze AI narratives in corporate filings and distinguish between substantive and speculative AI-related disclosures (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/392345572_Analyzing_the_market's_reaction_to_AI_narratives_in_corporate_filings). These studies provide the basis for analyzing whether markets and stakeholders differentiate between generic AI language and more credible, operationally grounded disclosure. I'll also use my previous knowledge on NLP/LLMs for language processing, quasi-causal methods, and general data science (I can provide more specific sources if necessary).
Empirical Methodology
The thesis will use a quantitative desk-research approach based on public data. I plan to collect 10-K and 10-Q filings from SEC EDGAR for U.S.-listed firms over 2021–2025 (to be scoped to 1-3 industries). Using NLP and large language model classification, I will build a dataset that allows the study of AI-related dimensions such as use-case specificity, operational implementation, quantification, governance, and risk disclosure, and then use clustering or dimensionality reduction techniques to find structure. After structure is found, and for answering the extended questions, timeseries analysis and quasi-causal techniques like DiD may be used. 
The empirical analysis will compare firms with vague pre-2024 AI disclosures to firms with more specific disclosures, before and after the SEC’s 2024 AI-washing scrutiny. The expected output is a practical disclosure-risk framework that managers, investor relations teams, and boards can use to benchmark and improve AI communication and potentially generate a generalized framework for future X-washing scenarios.
